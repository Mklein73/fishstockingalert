Pennsylvania Fishing License and Trout Permit Guide

The license alone does not cover trout, and the Lake Erie permit runs a lot further inland than most anglers assume.

Fish Stocking Alert • Regulations • 9 min read

Pennsylvania sells a fishing license and then sells several permits on top of it. Which ones you need depends on what you fish for and where, and the rules are not intuitive. The trout permit requirement in particular catches people who have fished the state for years.

This guide covers what each document does, who needs one, and the specific situations that generate citations.

Who Needs a License

Anglers 16 and older need a Pennsylvania fishing license. Anglers 15 and under fish free and need no license.

Youth under 16 may optionally buy a Voluntary Youth Fishing License for $2.97. It is not required. It supports PFBC youth programs, and it also serves as one of the two qualifying documents for Mentored Youth Trout Day.

Licenses go on sale December 1 each year and are valid through December 31 of the following year, which works out to 13 months of coverage from the December on-sale date. Multi-year options are available in 3, 5, and 10 year terms.

The Trout Permit

This is the one that matters most for anyone reading this site.

To fish for or keep trout, anglers 16 and older need both a fishing license and a current trout permit. The permit is separate and it costs extra. A fishing license alone does not cover trout.

Three specific situations to understand:

Stocked trout waters generally. If you are fishing for trout, you need the permit.

Delayed Harvest Artificial Lures Only sections. A current trout permit is required. This applies year round, including the zero-harvest months from the day after Labor Day through June 14 when you cannot keep anything.

Stocked Trout Waters Open to Year-Round Fishing. Here the rule splits. On rivers and streams in the program, a trout permit is required to fish at all. On lakes and ponds in the program, a trout permit is not required unless you take, kill, or possess a trout or salmon while fishing. So you can fish a year-round lake for bass or panfish without the permit, but the moment you keep a trout you need one.

Our year-round waters guide covers that program in detail.

The Lake Erie Permit

Fishing Lake Erie or its tributaries requires a Lake Erie Permit in addition to your fishing license. There is also a combination permit that covers both Lake Erie and inland trout, which is the right buy for anyone making a steelhead trip who might also fish an inland stocked stream on the same weekend.

The scope of the Lake Erie requirement is broader than most people assume. It covers Lake Erie, Presque Isle Bay, the peninsular waters, and the listed tributaries in their entirety, plus all waters that flow into those tributaries. The requirement does not stop a mile above the mouth. It runs to the headwaters and into every feeder creek.

The main permit-required tributaries are Cascade Creek, Conneaut Creek, Crooked Creek, Eightmile Creek, Elk Creek, Fourmile Creek, Mill Creek, Raccoon Creek, Sevenmile Creek, Sixmile Creek, Sixteenmile Creek, Turkey Creek, Twelvemile Creek, Twentymile Creek, and Walnut Creek.

Two exceptions. The East and West Basin ponds at Presque Isle State Park, including the Waterworks Ponds, do not require the permit. Inland ponds, lakes, and reservoirs in Erie County that are not part of a tributary system do not require it either.

Permit revenue funds access acquisition and easements on those tributaries, which is a reasonable argument for buying the combination permit even on an occasional trip. Our Lake Erie steelhead guide covers the fishery.

Other Permits and Add-Ons

PFBC has expanded its voluntary permit lineup in recent years. Alongside the required trout and Lake Erie permits, voluntary permits exist for several species and conservation purposes, including bass, musky, wild trout and enhanced waters, and habitat and waterways conservation. Voluntary permits are not required to fish. They fund specific management work.

Boaters should also note the separate launch permit and boat registration requirements, which are administered by PFBC but are not part of the fishing license.

Because the permit lineup and pricing change, verify current options and costs on the official PFBC license and permit page rather than relying on a figure you saw on a forum.

Where to Buy

Two routes.

Online through HuntFishPA. The PFBC online portal, also accessible through the FishBoatPA mobile app. You create an account, buy license and permits, and download the documents immediately. This is the faster option and it avoids the line at the tackle shop the week before opening day.

In person at a licensing agent. Roughly 700 retail agents across the state, mostly tackle shops, sporting goods stores, and some convenience stores. Worth using if you want to support a local shop or if you need help with the process.

Gift vouchers are available and can be redeemed later, which makes a fishing license a workable holiday gift.

Carrying and Displaying

While fishing, your license must be on your person. Pennsylvania removed the requirement to display it visibly, so you no longer need it pinned to a vest or hat.

You must be prepared to furnish positive proof of identification to a waterways conservation officer on request. A digital copy on your phone is generally acceptable, though a phone with a dead battery is not an excuse. Keeping a printed copy in a dry bag in your pack is cheap insurance.

Special Categories

  • Senior residents. Pennsylvania residents 65 and older qualify for reduced-cost annual licenses, and a resident senior lifetime license is available.
  • Multi-year licenses. Three, five, and ten year options for residents and non-residents.
  • Disabled veterans. May qualify for a free or reduced-cost license. Requirements and documentation are specified by PFBC.
  • Non-residents. Annual, multi-day, and one-day tourist licenses are available. If you are visiting for a steelhead weekend, compare the multi-day option against the annual.

Eligibility criteria change. Confirm on the PFBC site.

The Mistakes That Generate Citations

Fishing for trout with a license but no trout permit. The most common one. The license and the permit are separate products and a lot of people buy only the first.

Fishing an Erie tributary ten miles inland without a Lake Erie Permit. The requirement runs the full length of the tributary and into every feeder. Distance from the lake is irrelevant.

Fishing a Nursery Water. Trout Run, Godfrey Run, Orchard Beach Run, and Crooked Creek where posted in Erie County are designated Nursery Waters. It is unlawful to fish, wade, or possess fishing equipment while in or along them. No permit makes this legal.

Fishing stocked trout waters during the preseason closure. From roughly the third week of February until 8 a.m. on opening day, stocked trout waters are closed to all fishing. The exceptions are Mentored Youth Trout Day, waters in the year-round program, and Delayed Harvest sections.

Using bait on a Delayed Harvest section. Artificial lures, flies, and streamers only. Molded facsimiles of worms, insects, and fish eggs are specifically prohibited, which surprises anglers who assume a plastic egg counts as a lure.

Adults keeping trout on Mentored Youth Trout Day. Only the permitted youth may possess trout, and the limit is two. Our Mentored Youth guide covers the requirements.

Buying Timeline

December 1. Next year's licenses and permits go on sale. Buying now covers the remainder of the current year plus all of the next.

January and February. The quiet window. Buy online, get it done, and skip the March rush.

Two weeks before opening day. Lines at retail agents. Online is still instant.

October through December. PFBC has historically offered discounted pricing on select licenses during the fall in some years. Watch the PFBC newsroom if you are a late-season buyer.

Planning as a Non-Resident

Pennsylvania sells non-residents an annual license plus multi-day and one-day tourist options. Which one makes sense depends on trip count rather than trip length.

The break-even math is straightforward. Price the multi-day option against the annual and figure out how many trips you will realistically make. Anglers coming from Ohio, New York, New Jersey, and Maryland for a single steelhead weekend usually come out ahead on a multi-day license. Anyone making two or more trips a year is generally better off with the annual.

Two additions to budget for. If you are fishing Erie, you need the Lake Erie Permit on top of the license. If you are fishing inland trout on the same trip, the combination permit covering both is usually cheaper than buying them separately.

Check current non-resident pricing on the PFBC site before you plan, because the categories and costs change.

Reciprocity and Border Water

Pennsylvania borders six states plus a Great Lake, and boundary waters have their own arrangements that do not follow the general license rule.

The Delaware River, the Ohio border waters including Pymatuning Reservoir, Conowingo Reservoir on the Maryland line, and Lake Erie all carry specific provisions. Some allow anglers licensed in the neighboring state to fish from a boat, some require a Pennsylvania license regardless, and some apply different seasons and limits than the inland rules.

Do not assume your home state license covers you because you are on a border water. Look up the specific water in the current PFBC regulations summary before you fish it.

What the Money Funds

PFBC receives no general tax revenue. The agency runs on license and permit sales, boat registrations, and federal excise taxes on fishing tackle through the Sport Fish Restoration program.

That has a direct consequence for anglers. The 3.2 million trout PFBC stocks each year, the hatcheries that raise them, the access easements on the Erie tributaries, and the waterways conservation officers who enforce the regulations all come out of license and permit revenue.

It also explains the voluntary permits. When PFBC offers a voluntary bass, musky, wild trout, or habitat permit, it is asking anglers who care about a specific program to fund it directly. Nobody checks whether you bought one, and the work does not happen if not enough people do.

Keeping It Straight Across a Season

A simple routine avoids most license problems.

  • Buy in December. Licenses go on sale December 1 and cover through December 31 of the following year. Buying early gets you 13 months and no lines.
  • Buy the permits at the same time. Adding the trout permit later means a second transaction and a second thing to forget.
  • Download the documents immediately. Save the PDF to your phone and email it to yourself. Print a copy for the truck.
  • Check the expiration in November. A license that expires December 31 does not cover a late-December ice fishing trip if you bought it for the previous year.
  • Keep the customer number. It is nine digits, it is on every license you have ever bought, and it makes account recovery painless.

Quick Reference

  • License required. Anglers 16 and older. Under 16 fish free.
  • Trout permit. Required in addition to the license to fish for or keep trout.
  • Lake Erie Permit. Required for Lake Erie, Presque Isle Bay, and the listed tributaries in their entirety plus everything flowing into them.
  • Combination permit. Covers both Lake Erie and inland trout.
  • Year-round program split. Trout permit required to fish streams in the program. Required on lakes only if you keep a trout or salmon.
  • Validity. On sale December 1, valid through December 31 of the following year.
  • Carrying. Must be on your person. No display requirement. Be ready to show ID.
  • Where. HuntFishPA online, the FishBoatPA app, or roughly 700 retail agents.

Prices and permit categories change. Verify current costs and requirements with PFBC before you buy.
