Lake Erie Steelhead: A Guide to Pennsylvania’s Tributaries

Where the fish run, which permit you need, when each creek fishes best, and the rigs that produce from October through April.

Fish Stocking Alert • Location Guide • 11 min read

Every October, water temperatures in Lake Erie drop far enough that steelhead stack up off the creek mouths in Erie County and wait for rain. When the rain comes, they push into water you can wade in tennis shoes. Some of the best fishing in Pennsylvania happens in creeks you can throw a rock across.

The run holds from October through April. That is a long window, and the fishing changes completely between the first push in October and the spawning fish in March. This guide covers the tributaries, the permit rules that catch people off guard, and how to fish each stretch of the season.

Where Lake Erie Steelhead Come From

Pennsylvania's steelhead fishery is built, not inherited. The Fish and Boat Commission stocks over a million juvenile steelhead into Lake Erie tributaries each year. Those fish drop down to the lake, spend two to three years growing on an open-water forage base, and come back to the creek they were released in.

Natural reproduction in the Pennsylvania tributaries is limited. Most of these creeks go nearly dry in summer and lack the year-round cold water a wild steelhead population needs. The fish you catch in Elk Creek in November almost certainly came out of a hatchery truck three years earlier.

That matters for how you plan. Stocking locations drive return locations. Creeks that get the most smolts get the most returning adults, which is why Elk Creek and Walnut Creek carry the reputation and the crowds.

It also means the steelhead run does not appear in our Pennsylvania stocking data. Our Pennsylvania page tracks adult trout allocations and scheduled stocking dates for the statewide put-and-take program. Steelhead go in as juveniles under a separate program on a separate schedule. Treat this article as the Erie supplement to the rest of the state.

The Permit You Need Before You Cast

Anglers 16 and older need a Pennsylvania fishing license. For Lake Erie and its tributaries you also need a Lake Erie Permit. There is a combination permit that covers both Lake Erie and inland trout, which is what most people driving up from Pittsburgh or Philadelphia should buy, because you will probably fish an inland stocked stream on the same trip.

The permit requirement covers Lake Erie itself, Presque Isle Bay, the peninsular waters, and the tributaries in their entirety. That last part is where people get cited. The requirement is not just the lower half mile near the mouth. It runs all the way to the headwaters, and it extends to every water that flows into those tributaries. If you park on a back road ten miles inland and fish a feeder creek off Elk Creek, you still need the permit.

The main permit-required tributaries are Cascade Creek, Conneaut Creek, Crooked Creek, Eightmile Creek, Elk Creek, Fourmile Creek, Mill Creek, Raccoon Creek, Sevenmile Creek, Sixmile Creek, Sixteenmile Creek, Turkey Creek, Twelvemile Creek, Twentymile Creek, and Walnut Creek.

Two exceptions worth knowing. The East and West Basin ponds at Presque Isle State Park, including the Waterworks Ponds, do not require the Lake Erie Permit. Neither do inland ponds, lakes, and reservoirs in Erie County that are not tributary systems. Buy your license and permits through the PFBC license page before you leave home.

Lake Erie and its tributaries run under their own seasons, size limits, and creel limits, and those numbers are different from the inland trout regulations most Pennsylvania anglers have memorized. Check the current PFBC seasons, sizes, and creel limits page for the Lake Erie section before your first trip of the season. Do not assume last year's numbers carry over.

Nursery Waters Are Closed, and People Get Cited There Every Year

This is the single most expensive mistake a first-time Erie angler makes. Several waters are designated as Nursery Waters, and it is unlawful to fish, wade, or even possess fishing equipment while in or along them.

Trout Run, Godfrey Run, Orchard Beach Run, and Crooked Creek where posted are the designated Nursery Waters. Trout Run in particular is a magnet, because in October you can stand at the mouth and watch hundreds of steelhead stacked in the plume. Fishing the lake off the mouth of Trout Run is legal and productive. Walking up into the run is not, and neither is standing on the bank with a rod in your hand.

Watch for posted signs and respect them. The waterways conservation officers work these creeks hard in October and November because they know exactly where the fish and the crowds are.

The Tributaries, West to East

Elk Creek

Elk Creek is the longest tributary in the region and the one most anglers name first. It runs through Girard and Lake City in western Erie County and holds fish from the mouth up to the headwaters once flows come up. Length is the advantage. When Walnut is shoulder to shoulder, Elk gives you room to walk. The lower end near the mouth sees the heaviest pressure, and the middle stretches through the shale gorges thin out considerably if you are willing to hike.

Elk drops and clears faster than its size suggests because of the shale bottom. A rain event that has Elk running chocolate on Monday can have it in perfect shape by Wednesday.

Walnut Creek

Walnut Creek in Fairview Township is the most accessible tributary in the system and the most consistently crowded. The PFBC Walnut Creek Access Area at the marina gives you parking, restrooms, and immediate water. That convenience is exactly why you will not have it to yourself on a Saturday in November.

Walnut fishes well at night and in the first hour of light. If you want the access area on a weekend, plan to be standing in the water before sunrise. Weekday mornings in late November and December are a different experience entirely.

Twentymile, Sixteenmile, and Twelvemile Creeks

East of the city of Erie, this group of creeks gets meaningfully less pressure than Elk and Walnut. They are smaller, they blow out faster, and they drop faster. Twentymile Creek near North East runs through steep shale and offers deep plunge pools that hold fish through low-water periods when the flatter western creeks stop producing.

Access is tighter on the east side and private posting is common. Scout access on the PFBC maps before you drive, not after you arrive.

Conneaut Creek

Conneaut Creek is the far western option, running through Crawford and Erie counties before crossing into Ohio. It is a bigger, slower, siltier system than the shale creeks around Erie. Conneaut holds water longer after rain and stays fishable when the small east-side creeks have already dropped out. It also warms and colors up more, so read it differently.

Cascade, Mill, Fourmile, Sevenmile, and Sixmile Creeks

These are the small urban and suburban creeks in and immediately around the city of Erie. They are short, they need a good push of water to fish, and they clear in a hurry. Their value is timing. When a rain event has every major creek blown out, these small systems can be back in shape within a day while Elk is still unfishable.

Run Timing Month by Month

Late September and early October. Fish stage off the creek mouths in the lake. Water in the creeks is usually too low and too warm. Fish the mouths from shore, and watch the forecast.

Mid-October through November. Peak. The first significant rain event of the fall pulls large numbers of fish upstream at once. These are aggressive, chrome, freshly arrived fish, and they will chase. This is the window for streamers, spoons, and jigs. It is also the most crowded four to six weeks of the year.

December through February. Cold water, slow fish, and far fewer anglers. Steelhead pull into the deepest, slowest pools and hold. Presentations get smaller and drifts get slower. Ice shelves along the banks are a real hazard. This stretch of the season produces the best fish-per-angler ratio of the year if you can handle the conditions.

March and April. Spawning fish move onto gravel, and dropback fish head for the lake. Snowmelt keeps flows up. Dropback steelhead are thin but aggressive and will hammer a streamer on the swing. Leave fish on active spawning gravel alone.

Flow and Clarity Decide Everything

Steelhead fishing on the Erie tributaries is a weather sport. These creeks are small and flashy. A one-inch rain event takes them from a trickle to unfishable mud within hours, then back through a fishable window over the next one to three days.

The target is what everyone up there calls steelhead green. You want roughly one to two feet of visibility. The fish move, they cannot see you, and they will still find your bait. Gin-clear low water means spooky fish, long leaders, and a lot of walking. Chocolate water means go home or fish a smaller creek that has already dropped.

Check gauges before you commit to the drive. The same logic we cover in our guide to reading water conditions before you fish applies here, just compressed into a much shorter window.

Float Fishing Setup

Float fishing under a slip float is how most steelhead in Pennsylvania get caught, and it works for centerpin anglers and spinning anglers alike.

  • Rod. Ten to thirteen feet, light action. Length controls line and gives you drag-free drifts you cannot get with a seven-foot bass rod.
  • Line. Eight to ten pound main line, with a six to eight pound fluorocarbon leader. Drop to four pound fluorocarbon when the water is low and clear.
  • Float. Size it to the water. A quarter-ounce slip float handles most pools. Shot the float so only the tip rides above the surface, because a properly shotted float telegraphs the soft takes.
  • Hooks. Size 8 to 12 egg hooks for bait, size 10 to 14 for beads and single eggs.

Baits That Produce

Egg sacs are the standard. Tie them small, about the size of a nickel, in chartreuse and peach mesh. Chartreuse gets the nod in stained water and peach in clearer water. Single salmon eggs work when the fish have been pressured.

Trout beads have taken over a lot of the fishery. Eight to ten millimeter beads in peach, mottled orange, and a dead-egg cream color, pegged two inches above a bare hook, are hard to beat from November through February. Sucker spawn in white and pale yellow is a reliable second option under a float.

Marabou jigs from 1/64 to 1/16 ounce cover fish that will not commit to a dead drift. Black, white, olive, and pink are the four colors worth carrying. Fish them under the same float rig.

Fly Gear

A seven or eight weight with a nine to ten foot rod covers the tributaries. Switch and spey rods in the eleven to thirteen foot range are common on Elk and Conneaut where you have room to swing.

Nymphing under an indicator with egg patterns, sucker spawn, and stonefly nymphs is the highest percentage approach in cold water. Swing black and olive woolly buggers and small intruder-style streamers when fresh fish are pushing through in October and November and again for dropbacks in the spring.

Fluorocarbon tippet in six to eight pound is standard, dropping to four pound in low clear conditions. Bring more than you think, because shale and ice both eat leaders.

Access, Crowds, and Not Being That Guy

Access on the Erie tributaries is a patchwork of PFBC access areas, easements purchased with permit revenue, and private land. The permit money is what buys and maintains that access, which is a decent argument for buying the combination permit even if you only fish Erie twice a year.

Private posting changes year to year. Check current access maps rather than relying on where you parked three seasons ago. When in doubt, park in a designated access area and walk.

On etiquette, the norms up there are simple. Work through a pool from the top down rather than parking in one spot for two hours. Give the person below you room. If someone is fighting a fish, pull your line. Do not step into gravel with visible spawning fish on it in March.

Plan the Rest of Your Trip Around Fall Stockings

Most people who drive to Erie in October and November are going for one thing. The smarter play is to check what else is getting fish while you are up there. Pennsylvania runs a substantial fall stocking program that puts adult trout into lakes and stream sections from October into mid-December, and northwest Pennsylvania gets a share of it.

If the creeks blow out on day one of a weekend trip, an inland stocked stream or lake that took fish the week before will save the weekend. Our guide to fishing Pennsylvania stocked trout in fall covers which waters get fall fish, how the extended season limits work, and how to time a trip around a stocking date.

Quick Reference

  • Run window. October through April, with the heaviest push mid-October through November.
  • Permit. Fishing license plus Lake Erie Permit, required on the tributaries in their entirety and everything flowing into them.
  • Closed water. Trout Run, Godfrey Run, Orchard Beach Run, and Crooked Creek where posted are Nursery Waters. No fishing, no wading, no equipment.
  • Best conditions. One to two feet of visibility, one to three days after a rain event.
  • Highest pressure. Walnut Creek access area and the lower mile of Elk Creek, weekends in November.
  • Regulations. Lake Erie seasons, sizes, and creel limits are separate from inland trout rules. Verify current numbers with PFBC before you go.
