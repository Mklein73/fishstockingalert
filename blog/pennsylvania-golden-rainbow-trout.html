Golden Rainbow Trout in Pennsylvania: Where and When

About 18,000 go in each year against 3.2 million standard trout, and three quarters of them are stocked before opening day.

Fish Stocking Alert • Species Guide • 9 min read

You see one flash yellow in a pool and the whole day changes. Golden rainbow trout are the most recognizable fish Pennsylvania stocks, and they are rare enough that catching one still feels like an event.

PFBC stocks approximately 18,000 golden rainbows a year against roughly 3.2 million total adult trout. That works out to about one golden fish in every 175 trout stocked. Knowing where and when they go in narrows those odds considerably.

What a Golden Rainbow Actually Is

Golden rainbows are rainbow trout with a color mutation, not a separate species and not a hybrid. The strain traces back to a single unusually colored rainbow trout found at a West Virginia hatchery in the 1950s, selectively bred from there and distributed to hatcheries across the region.

Anglers call them palominos, goldens, banana trout, and lightning trout depending on the county. PFBC calls them golden rainbow trout. Some anglers assume they are a golden trout, which is a different fish entirely and native to California. Pennsylvania does not stock those.

The color is the whole story for the fishery. A golden rainbow in clear water is visible from thirty yards, which makes them fun and makes them vulnerable.

Size and Numbers

PFBC stocks approximately 18,000 golden rainbow trout annually. They average 1.5 pounds and measure at least 14 inches, which puts them well above the 11 inch, .58 pound average for the standard adult stocking.

These are trophy-class fish by stocked trout standards. A 20 inch golden rainbow shows up in Pennsylvania waters every year.

One accounting detail matters if you read stocking data closely. PFBC counts golden rainbow trout within its rainbow trout totals rather than as a separate line. When you see a rainbow trout allocation number for a section, the goldens are typically already inside it. Do not add the two together and assume you found extra fish.

The Timing That Decides Everything

Roughly 75 percent of Pennsylvania's golden rainbows go in before opening day. The remaining 25 percent go in during in-season stockings.

That split shapes the entire strategy. Three quarters of the annual golden allocation is sitting in the water on opening morning, and a large share of those fish get caught in the first two weekends because they are impossible to miss.

Two windows follow from that:

  • Opening day and the first week. Maximum golden density. Also maximum crowd. If you want the best raw odds, this is it.
  • In-season stockings from late April into June. The other 25 percent trickle in when pressure has dropped hard. Fewer goldens in the water, far fewer anglers looking for them.

Fall stockings can include goldens as well, though the fall program is much smaller overall. Our guide to fishing Pennsylvania stocked trout in fall covers that window.

How to Find Them in the Data

Golden rainbows are not spread evenly. PFBC allocates them by section, which means some stream sections and lakes receive them and others do not.

Our Pennsylvania data tracks golden rainbow allocations at the section level, which is the practical way to answer the question of where they go. Two things to keep straight when you read it. The count is the annual planned allocation for that section across the whole year, not a per-event number. The scheduled date is a separate fact. A section planned to receive golden rainbows in a given year, with a stocking scheduled the week of March 16, is not a section that receives all of them on March 16.

All stockings are subject to change without notice, and hatchery logistics move goldens around more than standard fish because they come from specific production lots.

Where Goldens Tend to Show Up

Beyond the section data, a few patterns hold across the state.

High-visibility waters. PFBC puts goldens where people will see them. Community lakes, park water, and stream sections with heavy walk-in access get a disproportionate share. This is deliberate. A yellow trout in a park pond creates more angler interest than the same fish in a remote gorge.

Mentored Youth waters. Sections stocked ahead of Mentored Youth Trout Day often carry goldens, because the whole point of the day is to give kids a memorable fish. Our Mentored Youth Trout Day guide covers how that day works.

Lakes over streams. Goldens hold in lakes better than in small streams. In a shallow creek, a bright yellow fish gets found by every angler and every heron within a week. In twelve feet of water it survives.

How to Catch One

Fish Them Like a Rainbow, Because That Is What They Are

Golden rainbows eat what rainbows eat. There is no special golden trout bait. What changes is the approach, because you can usually see the fish before you cast.

Sight Fishing

The color cuts both ways. You can spot a golden holding in a pool from well upstream, which means you can plan a cast instead of blind-fishing. It also means the fish has been spotted by everyone else who walked that bank, and by day three it has refused a dozen presentations.

Approach from downstream and stay low. Make the first cast count, because a golden that gets spooked in a small pool relocates and stops feeding for an hour.

Presentations

In the first 48 hours after a stocking, a chartreuse or rainbow dough bait on a size 12 or 14 hook under enough split shot to hold bottom is the highest percentage approach where bait is legal. Single salmon eggs and small pieces of nightcrawler work equally well.

After the first week, switch to something that moves. Inline spinners in size 1 with gold blades, 1/16 ounce marabou jigs in white and olive under a float, and small spoons on a slow retrieve. A golden that has refused stationary bait for four days will still chase.

On fly gear, olive woolly buggers in size 10, beadhead pheasant tails in size 14 to 16, and San Juan worms in red. Nothing exotic.

Timing Within the Day

Low light helps you and hurts your ability to spot fish. Early morning gets the least-pressured feeding window, and the fish are less spooky. Overcast days are ideal, because goldens hold in shallower water when there is no bright sun and they feed longer.

Keeping One, or Not

Golden rainbows count toward your regular trout creel limit. They are not separately regulated. During the regular season the limit is five trout combined species at a seven inch minimum, and during the extended season it drops to three. Special regulation waters override those numbers.

A lot of anglers release them, partly for the next person and partly because a 1.5 pound hatchery rainbow is not exactly table fare worth bragging about. That is a personal call. If you keep one, keep one, not four.

Verify current limits on the PFBC trout fishing regulations page.

Lakes Hold Them Longer Than Streams

Where a golden rainbow gets stocked determines how long it lasts, and the difference is dramatic.

In a small stream, a bright yellow fish in clear water is visible to every angler who walks the bank and every heron and kingfisher in the drainage. Goldens stocked into shallow creeks tend to get caught or eaten within the first two weeks.

In a lake with twelve feet of water, the same fish has somewhere to go. Goldens in stocked lakes routinely survive into the following season, and a carryover golden that has been eating real forage for a year is an impressive fish.

If your goal is a big golden rather than any golden, target stocked lakes and the deeper stream sections rather than the small water where they are easiest to spot.

Common Misconceptions

They are not golden trout. The California golden trout is a separate species and Pennsylvania does not stock it. A golden rainbow is a rainbow trout with a color mutation.

They are not sterile or hybrid. They are rainbow trout, capable of reproducing, which almost none of them get the chance to do in Pennsylvania put-and-take water.

They do not taste different. The flesh is standard hatchery rainbow. The color is skin deep.

They are not protected. Goldens count toward your standard trout creel limit with no separate rules. Some anglers assume there is a special regulation because the fish is unusual. There is not.

They are not always easy. A freshly stocked golden will eat almost anything. A golden that has held in the same pool for two weeks and been cast to fifty times is one of the harder fish in the section.

Photographing and Releasing

Most people who catch a golden want a picture, which is fair, and the picture is where a lot of these fish get killed.

Keep the fish in the water while you get the camera ready rather than after. Wet your hands. Support it horizontally with one hand under the belly rather than hanging it vertically by the jaw. Ten seconds out of the water, not ninety.

Revive it facing into current until it swims off under its own power. In warm water, above about 68 degrees, consider skipping the photo entirely, because the combination of a long fight, warm water, and air exposure kills a high percentage of released trout.

If you are keeping it, dispatch it promptly and get it on ice. A golden rainbow left on a stringer in the sun for four hours is a waste of a fish that PFBC spent two years growing.

Quick Reference

  • Annual stocking. Approximately 18,000 golden rainbow trout.
  • Size. Average 1.5 pounds, at least 14 inches.
  • Timing. About 75 percent before opening day, about 25 percent in-season.
  • Odds. Roughly one golden per 175 trout stocked statewide.
  • Data note. PFBC counts goldens inside its rainbow trout totals. Do not double count.
  • Best odds. Opening weekend on a section with a golden allocation, or in-season stockings once the crowd leaves.
  • Limits. Count toward the standard trout creel limit. No separate golden regulation.
