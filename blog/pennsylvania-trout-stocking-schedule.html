The Pennsylvania Trout Stocking Schedule, Explained

What the PFBC schedule tells you, what it does not, and how to turn a county list into a plan for the whole season.

Fish Stocking Alert • Planning • 10 min read

PFBC publishes a trout stocking schedule every January, and most anglers use maybe ten percent of what is in it. They look up one county, find their creek, note a date, and close the tab. The schedule supports a lot more than that.

This guide covers what the schedule actually contains, what it does not, and how to turn a county list into a plan for the season.

What Gets Published and When

PFBC posts the adult trout stocking schedule in late January. The 2026 schedule went up on January 29.

The schedule is searchable by county, lists waterways alphabetically, and shows stocking dates, meeting locations for volunteers, and the species planned for each location. It is available on the PFBC site and through the FishBoatPA mobile app.

A separate fall and winter announcement typically comes in late September, covering the October through mid-December stockings. Those waters are a much smaller subset. Our fall stocking guide covers that program.

The Scale You Are Working With

For 2026, PFBC planned approximately 3.2 million adult trout into 684 streams and 130 lakes open to public angling. The species breakdown is roughly 2.3 million rainbow trout, 698,000 brown trout, and 128,000 brook trout. Average stocked size is 11 inches at .58 pounds.

On top of that:

  • Approximately 72,000 trophy-sized brood fish at 14 to 20 inches, 2.5 and 3.5 years old.
  • Approximately 18,000 golden rainbow trout averaging 1.5 pounds, with about 75 percent going in before opening day.
  • Approximately 10,000 large trout at 14 to 20 inches through the Keystone Select program, spread across 24 stream sections.
  • Roughly 1.1 million additional trout from cooperative nurseries run by sportsmen's clubs and other groups.

Counting that up, the schedule is describing the movement of a lot of fish into a lot of places over ten months. Picking well matters more than most anglers assume.

The Two Facts People Merge, and Should Not

This is the most important thing to understand about Pennsylvania stocking data, ours included.

Planned allocation counts and stocking dates are separate facts.

PFBC publishes planned fish counts by species for a stream section, and those counts are annual figures covering every stocking that section receives across the whole year. PFBC also publishes stocking dates. It does not publish per-event counts, because the agency does not track and release fish numbers per individual truck delivery.

So a section with a planned allocation of 1,200 rainbow trout and a stocking scheduled the week of October 12 is not a section that receives 1,200 rainbow trout on October 12. It is a section planned to receive 1,200 rainbow trout across the year, with one of its stockings scheduled the week of October 12.

Say it the correct way and you keep your expectations calibrated. Say it the wrong way and you drive somewhere expecting a truckload that was never coming.

A second wrinkle. Not every scheduled date is exact. Most records carry a specific date, and a small share are week-of approximations. Treat a week-of record as a three to five day window rather than a commitment.

And the standing caveat that PFBC repeats every year: all stockings are subject to change without notice. Water temperature swings and hatchery logistics move things around, sometimes by more than a week.

Reading a Section Correctly

Pennsylvania organizes stocked streams into numbered sections, not whole waterways. A single creek can have four sections with different boundaries, different allocations, and different regulations.

That is why the section number matters. Bald Eagle Creek in Centre County is the clean example. Section 03 is a Keystone Select water managed under a Miscellaneous Special Regulation with all tackle types permitted. Other sections of the same creek are standard stocked trout water with standard rules. Same name on the map, completely different fishing.

When you look up a water, check the section boundaries against where you actually plan to park. Boundaries are described by bridges, road crossings, tributary confluences, and property lines, and they get revised year to year.

Use Fish Per Mile, Not Total Count

The single most useful transformation you can apply to stocking data is dividing the planned allocation by the section length.

A section with 2,200 planned trout across nine miles works out to about 244 fish per mile. A section with 800 across two miles is 400 per mile. The second section has fewer than half the fish and fishes considerably better.

For reference, Keystone Select sections receive their large trout at 175 to 225 fish per mile, and PFBC describes that rate as comparable to the numbers of similarly sized fish in Pennsylvania's best wild trout waters. That is a useful benchmark when you are evaluating a standard section.

Read the Species Column

The species mix on a section changes how you fish it.

  • Rainbow trout dominate at roughly 2.3 million of the 3.2 million total. They hold in open water, school more, and hit bright offerings. A rainbow-heavy section fishes fast in the first week.
  • Brown trout at roughly 698,000 are the carryover species. They go to cover, feed at low light, and are the fish most likely to still be there next spring at a larger size.
  • Brook trout at roughly 128,000 are the smallest allocation. Sections that get them tend to be colder, higher, and smaller.
  • Golden rainbows are counted inside the rainbow trout totals rather than separately. Do not add them on top or you will double count. Our golden rainbow guide explains.

Check the Regulation Flag Before Anything Else

A section's regulation determines whether your tackle is legal and whether you can keep a fish. Check it first, then check the stocking date.

  • Standard stocked trout water. Closed from around the third week of February until 8 a.m. opening day. Five trout at seven inches in the regular season, three in the extended season.
  • Delayed Harvest Artificial Lures Only. Open year-round. Artificial lures, flies, and streamers only. Harvest June 15 through Labor Day, three fish at nine inches. Zero the rest of the year. Our delayed harvest guide covers it.
  • Keystone Select. Delayed Harvest rules on 23 of 24 sections, plus large trout at 14 to 20 inches stocked in spring. See our Keystone Select guide.
  • Stocked Trout Waters Open to Year-Round Fishing. Open every day, three-tier limit structure. Our year-round guide covers it.
  • Catch and release sections in artificial lures only, fly-fishing only, and all-tackle variants. No harvest at any time.

Building a Season Plan

Here is how to use the schedule across a full year rather than one weekend.

January

The schedule posts. Pull your county, note every section within your drive radius, and record the planned dates. Buy your license and trout permit while the lines are short. Our license and permit guide covers what you need.

Mid-February to Opening Day

Stocking begins and standard waters close. Fish the Delayed Harvest sections and year-round waters, which stay open. Use the closure to scout your opening day section on foot.

Opening Day Through May

Maximum fish, maximum pressure. Our opening day guide covers planning the day.

Late May Through June

In-season stockings continue while the crowd disappears. Check the schedule for in-season dates on your sections. This is the most underused window in the schedule.

July and August

Stocking stops on most water. Shift to tailwaters, limestone-influenced streams, and elevation.

Late September

The fall and winter schedule posts. A much shorter list, so read all of it rather than just your county.

October Through Mid-December

Fall stocking. Fish can be caught the same day they go in, with no closed period. Weighted toward lakes, especially in November and December.

Practical Habits

  • Check close to your trip. A date noted in January can move by the time March arrives.
  • Use the volunteer listings. The schedule includes meeting locations for stocking volunteers. Helping stock a section is the fastest way to learn where the deep water is.
  • Track more than one section. Postponements happen. Having a second option 20 minutes away saves the day.
  • Set alerts instead of checking manually. The schedule does not notify you when something changes. Our alerts do.

The authoritative source is always the PFBC trout stocking page. Our data is built on it and organized for planning rather than lookup.

What the Schedule Does Not Tell You

Knowing the limits of the data saves you from bad assumptions.

It does not tell you how many fish are in the water right now. It tells you what was planned and when a truck was scheduled. Between angler harvest, predation, and fish moving out of a section, the population on any given Saturday is a guess.

It does not tell you fish size beyond the averages. The statewide average is 11 inches at .58 pounds. Whether your section got brood fish at 14 to 20 inches is not published section by section.

It does not tell you access conditions. A section can be listed and stocked while most of the bank is posted private. Stocking limits get revised specifically because of landowner posting, which is why several waters were shortened or removed for 2026.

It does not cover cooperative nursery fish. Sportsmen's clubs and other groups raise roughly 1.1 million additional trout a year and stock them into public water on their own schedules. Those fish are real and they are not in the state schedule. If a section fishes better than its allocation suggests, a co-op nursery is often the reason.

Handling Postponements

Stockings move. Water temperature swings, high flows, and hatchery logistics all push dates around, sometimes by more than a week. PFBC states plainly that schedules are subject to change and asks anglers to check often.

Three habits keep a postponement from wasting a day off. Check the schedule again within 48 hours of your trip. Keep a backup section within a 20 minute drive of your first choice. And remember that a postponed stocking is not a cancelled one, so a section that got skipped in March usually gets its fish a week or two later with nobody watching.

Volunteering Is the Best Scouting There Is

The schedule lists meeting locations for stocking volunteers alongside the dates. Showing up to help carry buckets is the fastest way to learn a section, because you walk the access points with the crew and you see exactly where the fish go in.

PFBC welcomes public volunteers. Smaller groups of up to four generally do not need to register. Larger groups should coordinate in advance with the regional law enforcement office. Follow the truck safely and obey traffic laws, which sounds obvious and is the thing crews mention every year.

Quick Reference

  • Published. Adult schedule in late January. Fall and winter schedule in late September.
  • Scale. Approximately 3.2 million adult trout, 684 streams, 130 lakes.
  • Counts. Annual planned allocations per section, not per-event actuals.
  • Dates. Mostly exact, a small share are week-of approximations. All subject to change without notice.
  • Best metric. Fish per mile. Benchmark against the 175 to 225 per mile Keystone Select rate.
  • Check first. The section's regulation, before the stocking date.
