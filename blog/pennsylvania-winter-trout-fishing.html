Fishing Stocked Trout in Winter in Pennsylvania

The season does not end at Labor Day, and the water that stays open holds the highest trout density of the year.

Fish Stocking Alert • Technique • 10 min read

Pennsylvania trout season does not end at Labor Day. The extended season runs into February, PFBC stocks lakes in November and December specifically to set up ice fishing, and Delayed Harvest and year-round waters stay open every day of the year.

Winter fishing here is a small-numbers game with good odds. Fewer fish are feeding, but you have the water to yourself and the trout that do eat are often the biggest ones in the section.

What Is Legal in Winter

Three categories of water are open, and the rules differ on each.

Standard Stocked Trout Waters, Extended Season

The extended season begins the day after Labor Day and runs into February. For the current cycle, that window runs from September 8, 2026 through February 15, 2027. The daily limit is three trout of combined species with a seven inch minimum, down from five during the regular season.

After the extended season ends, standard stocked trout waters close to all fishing until 8 a.m. on the opening day of trout season.

Delayed Harvest Artificial Lures Only

Open year-round with no closed season. From the day after Labor Day until June 15 the daily creel limit is zero, so winter fishing there is catch and release. Tackle is restricted to artificial lures made of metal, plastic, rubber, or wood, plus flies and streamers. No natural bait, no fish eggs natural or molded.

This is the most reliable winter option in the state, because nobody has removed a fish from that section since Labor Day. Our delayed harvest guide covers the details.

Stocked Trout Waters Open to Year-Round Fishing

Open every day. Three trout at seven inches during the extended season, then catch and immediate release from the third Monday in February until opening day. A trout permit is required to fish streams in this program, and required on lakes only if you keep a trout or salmon. Our year-round waters guide covers which waters qualify.

Verify current dates and limits on the PFBC trout fishing regulations page.

The November and December Lake Stockings

PFBC times a specific block of stockings for winter. In the 2025 cycle, roughly 25 lakes received about 28,600 trout across November and December, on top of the larger October round. Those fish are put in place deliberately so they are settled and distributed before ice forms.

The practical implication is that a lake stocked the second week of December is not a December bank fishing plan so much as a January and February ice fishing plan. The fish are there either way, but they spend those first weeks spreading out and finding depth.

How Trout Behave in Cold Water

Everything slows down. A trout's metabolism drops with water temperature, and below about 40 degrees they eat less often and move much shorter distances to do it.

Three consequences drive winter tactics.

They stop chasing. A spinner ripped through a pool in July draws a strike. The same retrieve in January gets ignored. Presentations have to come to the fish, slowly, at their depth.

They stack up. Trout concentrate in the deepest, slowest water available. On a stream that means the bottom third of the deepest pools. On a lake it means the deepest basin or the deepest structure they can find. You can walk past a hundred yards of empty water and then find the entire population in one hole.

They feed in a narrow window. The warmest part of the day matters more in winter than at any other time. On a January stream, the difference between 10 a.m. and 2 p.m. can be the difference between zero and a good afternoon. Skip the dawn patrol. Sleep in.

Stream Tactics

Find the Deep Slow Water

Winter trout want depth and minimal current. Look for the deepest holes, the slow tailouts below them, undercut banks, and any spot where a spring seep enters. Spring water runs around 50 degrees year round, and a seep entering a 36 degree stream creates a warm pocket that concentrates fish.

Snow-covered banks make seeps easy to find. Look for open water and bare ground where everything else is white.

Slow Everything Down

The single most common winter mistake is fishing too fast. Cut your retrieve speed in half, then cut it again. On a jig under a float, let the float drift at exactly current speed with no drag at all. On a spinner, use the slowest retrieve that keeps the blade turning.

Go Small and Get Deep

  • Marabou jigs in 1/64 and 1/32 ounce under a small float. Black, olive, white, and pink. This is the highest percentage winter presentation on a stream, and it works with a spinning rod.
  • Nymphs in size 16 to 20. Beadhead pheasant tails, hare's ears, zebra midges, and small stoneflies. Add enough weight to tick bottom.
  • Small spinners in size 0 and 1, retrieved just fast enough to spin.
  • Streamers for the biggest fish. Olive and black woolly buggers in size 8, stripped very slowly or dead drifted through the deep slot.

Light Tippet

Winter water is low and clear. Drop to 5X or 6X fluorocarbon on fly gear, and 4 pound fluorocarbon on spinning gear. Carryover trout in a Delayed Harvest section in January have seen everything.

Lake and Ice Tactics

On open water, fish the deepest accessible structure. A slip float rig set to suspend bait or a jig one to two feet off the bottom in 10 to 20 feet of water covers most winter lake situations. Fish it dead still. Twitch it once every thirty seconds.

Through the ice, stocked trout suspend more than most species. Do not assume they are on bottom. Work the column from just under the ice down to the basin and pay attention to where the marks are.

  • Small tungsten jigs tipped with a waxworm or a small piece of nightcrawler.
  • Spoons in 1/16 to 1/8 ounce, jigged sharply and then held still. The pause is when the strike comes.
  • Tip-ups with a small live minnow set eight to ten feet down.
  • Dough bait on a small treble under a float in an open hole, which is as effective through ice as it is from shore.

Safety

Winter fishing in Pennsylvania carries real risk, and the risk is cold water rather than the fish.

Ice. No ice is guaranteed safe. Pennsylvania winters swing hard, and a lake that held six inches in January can be unsafe in a warm week. Check thickness as you go, never fish alone on new ice, and carry ice picks around your neck.

Wading. Falling in at 35 degrees is a different event than falling in at 65. Wear a wading belt, use a staff, and stay out of water above your knees when you are alone. Ice shelves along the bank collapse without warning.

Boating. From November 1 through April 30, Pennsylvania requires anyone on a boat under 16 feet, including all canoes, kayaks, and paddleboards, to wear a life jacket. Children 12 and under must always wear one while paddling.

Layers. Wool or synthetic against the skin, never cotton. Bring a dry change in the truck. Hand warmers in the chest pocket of your waders do more good than in your gloves.

Best Winter Water in Pennsylvania

  • Delayed Harvest sections. Highest fish density in the state during winter, because harvest has been closed since Labor Day.
  • Tailwaters. Bottom releases from dams hold temperature. The Tulpehocken below Blue Marsh, Pohopoco below Beltzville, and the Lackawaxen below Wallenpaupack all stay fishable when freestone streams ice over.
  • Year-round lakes. Especially the ones that received November and December stockings.
  • Limestone-influenced streams. Spring input keeps water temperature more stable through winter.

Reading Winter Flows

Winter water behaves differently than the rest of the year, and the same gauge reading means something else in January than it does in May.

Low and clear is the default in a cold snap, because precipitation is locked up as snow and ice. Fish are stacked in the deepest water, they can see everything, and you need light tippet and long drifts.

Rising and cold after a snowmelt event is the worst combination. Melt water runs colder than the stream it enters, which drops temperature and shuts fish down entirely. The day after a thaw begins is usually poor.

Rising and warming after a winter rain is the best window of the season. Water temperature climbing two or three degrees turns fish on hard. If the forecast shows a 50 degree rain in February, take the day off.

Slush and anchor ice make a stream effectively unfishable regardless of what the gauge says. Anchor ice forming on the bottom pushes trout out of their normal lies and into deep slow pools, so if you find open water, fish it thoroughly.

Gear That Fails in the Cold

Winter fishing problems are usually equipment problems rather than fish problems.

  • Guides icing up. The most common issue on a fly rod or a spinning rod below freezing. Lip balm or a commercial paste on the guides helps. Keeping the rod out of the water between casts helps more.
  • Reel drags freezing. Water inside a reel turns to ice. Keep the reel out of the stream when you land a fish and dry it before you put it away.
  • Fluorocarbon stiffening. Cold makes tippet coil and behave badly. Stretch it before you tie on.
  • Wet gloves. Fingerless wool gloves plus a chest pocket with hand warmers beats waterproof gloves that trap sweat.
  • Cotton anything. Wet cotton against skin in freezing weather is a genuine hypothermia risk. Wool or synthetic only.
  • Frozen boot laces and studs. Felt soles ice up badly on snow. Rubber soles with studs handle it better.

Keep a dry change of clothes and a way to make heat in the vehicle. A thermos of something hot does more for a winter day than any piece of tackle.

Handling Fish in Freezing Weather

Cold water holds oxygen well, which means winter trout recover from a fight faster than summer trout do. Air is the problem instead.

Below freezing, exposed gill tissue can be damaged in seconds. Keep the fish in the water. Unhook it in the net without lifting it out. Skip the photo unless you can take it with the fish partly submerged.

Wet hands freeze fast in the wind, which is one more reason to use hemostats and barbless hooks and to make the whole process take under ten seconds.

Quick Reference

  • Extended season. September 8, 2026 through February 15, 2027. Three trout, seven inch minimum.
  • After the extended season. Standard stocked waters close until opening day. Delayed Harvest and year-round waters stay open.
  • Best time of day. Late morning through mid-afternoon, the warmest hours.
  • Best presentation. 1/64 to 1/32 ounce marabou jig under a float, drifted at current speed.
  • Best water. Deepest, slowest holding water, and anywhere a spring seep enters.
  • Life jacket rule. Required November 1 through April 30 on boats under 16 feet.
